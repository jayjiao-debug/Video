## Story-worthy (top 5)

1. **31 m free fall, 2.49 s real, remembered as 36% longer (Stetson et al. 2007).** People who did the jump could not read a fast-flashing number any faster mid-fall (p = 0.86). Time did not slow down; the memory got denser. Visual idea: two stopwatches, others' fall at 2.17 s (under the real 2.49 s) and own fall at 2.96 s.
2. **Janet's ratio, put in the viewer's own numbers.** At age 10 a year is 1/10 of your life, at 50 it is 1/50 (Janet 1877 via James 1890). For the audience: at 18 it is 5.6%, at 25 it is 4%. An easy shrinking-bar visual.
3. **Only "the last ten years" speeds up.** 1,865 people aged 16–80 all said time feels fast. Age made almost no difference for a week, a month or a year, only for the past decade (Friedman & Janssen 2010; Wittmann & Lehnhoff 2005, N = 499, aged 14–94, plateau around 50). It is a twist: what changes is the memory look-back, not the clock.
4. **Routine shrinks remembered time.** People who counted the same digit "5" over and over remembered the session as about 23% shorter than people who counted different digits. At Club Med, 51% said the last part of the holiday felt shortest (Avni-Babad & Ritov 2003). James in 1890: routine makes "the years grow hollow and collapse".
5. **A fever speeds up the inner clock.** Hoagland's wife, sick with flu, counted to "60" in about 35 s at the highest temperatures instead of about 55 s (Hoagland 1933, plotted in Wearden). A good opener for the idea that the brain has its own adjustable clock.

Runner-up: in a stream of identical images, the odd one out can feel up to 50% longer (Tse et al. 2004, via Pariyadath & Eagleman 2007). Earliest memories date to about 3.2 years on average, and about 40% of people "remember" age 2 or younger, probably fictionally (Akhtar et al. 2018).

## Risks

- **Overclaiming the age effect.** The data do NOT say older people feel every day or week go faster. Effects are small (≤10% of variance) and show up mainly for the past decade. Suggested wording: 回头看"过去十年"，年纪越大越觉得快. Avoid 老人觉得每天都过得飞快.
- **Janet's ratio is a 19th-century idea, not a measured law.** James himself rejected it as an elementary law. Present it as 一种解释/比例理论, not 科学证明.
- **Bejan is an essay or hypothesis.** It has no new data and is controversial. Label it 一位物理学家的假说.
- **The Hoagland numbers are approximate.** The exact "34 s at 103 °F" or "37 s" figures have no credible source I could open. Use 约35秒 / 只用了半分钟多 / 快了近一半 with the n = 1 caveat. Do not give a °C figure more precise than about 39 °C (103 °F = 39.4 °C).
- **Ornstein experiment numbers are unverified.** Use the hypothesis only. Reviews call the support "inconsistent", and the contextual-change model is the modern version.
- **The oddball "50%" is a maximum ("up to") and applies to sub-second flashes.** Do not imply that a whole new day feels 50% longer.
- **The holiday paradox comes from a popular book (Hammond 2012).** Cite the idea to James (1890) and to Avni-Babad & Ritov's vacation study if you need a scientific anchor.
- **Stetson's duration-judgment sample is tiny** (n = 7 for that measure). The 36% is a mean with a large SD (±0.51 s).
- **Day-of-year convention.** 76.2% versus 76.4% depends on whether today is counted. Rounding to "约76% / 四分之三" avoids the issue. If the video publishes on 10-08, use 281st day / about 77%.
- **Primary-source access gaps.** I could not open Janet (1877), Hoagland (1933), Ornstein (1969) or the full texts of Tse (2004) and Wittmann & Lehnhoff (2005). Their numbers rest on the named secondary sources.
