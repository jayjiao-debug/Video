## 1. Most surprising, story-worthy facts (top 5)

1. **None of the words were real.** In "How good is your sweardar?", 215 people were told that one word in each
   pair was a swear word. Unknown to them, every word was a pseudo-word. They still picked the same kind of word:
   the one **without** l / r / w / y, on **63 %** of trials (chance 50 %, p < .001). (L3, L4, L6)
2. **The voices were machines.** The stimuli were made with text-to-speech, and both words in a pair had the same
   tone and stress. So listeners were judging the sound of the word, not anger. This is exactly the film's
   round 2 (calm ElevenLabs voice). (L5)
3. **The French result runs against their own language.** French swear words are full of r and l (the paper's
   point; the press coverage gives *merde*, *salaud*, *connard*). Yet French speakers showed **numerically the
   strongest** effect of the six groups. (L8, L9)
4. **To soften a swear word, people add an l / r / w / y.** In 67 English minced oaths (like *darn* for *damn*),
   there are **29** approximants against **12** in the original swear words (p < .001). (L11)
5. **Swearing really helps against pain, but only real swear words.** Repeating a swear word kept hands in ice
   water longer: in 2009, men lasted about 191 s against 147 s, women about 120 s against 83 s. Made-up "swear
   words" (*fouch*, *twizpipe*) did **nothing** (2020, pre-registered). The punch comes from what you learned,
   not from the sound alone. This is the honest counterweight to facts 1-4. (P1, P3)

**Strongest human story.** In 1861 Paul Broca examined Leborgne, known in the hospice as "Tan". He could say only
the syllable *tan, tan*. But "he would easily become enraged, and then add to his vocabulary a great swearword,
one only" (Broca's own report). He lost language but kept the swear word. (B1)

**Best visual image.** One Albanian word, *zog* ("bird"), split into two fakes: **yog** (approximant) and **tsog**
(affricate). Show them as two waveforms: the smooth glide of y- against the burst of ts-. 63 % pointed at tsog.
For a Chinese audience, the paper's own Chinese pair is **gou → rou / chou** (L7). The second image is the ice-water
bucket with a stopwatch, "fuck" against *fouch*.

## 2. Caveats the film must respect (read before writing)

1. **63 % is "more often than chance", not "almost everyone".** The study measured how often people picked the
   word without an approximant. It did not score them "right": there was no real swear word in the pairs. Safe
   wording: 「10次里，有6次多选了同一个」 / 「明显超过瞎猜的50%」. Don't write "大多数人都猜对了" about the paper. If our
   own viewers do well in round 1, that is the **tone** (angry voice), not this paper.
2. **The paper used pseudo-words, not real swear words.** If the film plays real foreign swear words, the paper does
   not back up "you could tell from the sounds". Many real swear words contain approximants (French). Best: build
   round 2 exactly like the paper, from fake word pairs (e.g. the paper's own pairs, Table 1, row L7).
3. **The authors' own caveats (L12):** (a) it is about **perceptions** of swearing, not swearing itself; (b)
   "probabilistic rather than deterministic", so an l or r does not make a word inoffensive; (c) all listeners
   knew English, so English sound patterns might explain part of it (the French result argues against this).
   "As to the wider universe, the jury is out." Don't say "全世界脏话都一样" or "科学证明脏话有通用发音".
4. **The swear-word-sound pattern itself is weak.** In the pilot count of real swear words in 5 languages, the
   shortage of approximants was **not significant** after correction (L2). Plosives (p t k b d g) were **not**
   over-represented. So don't claim "swear words are full of hard sounds like k and t". The evidence is about what
   swear words **avoid** (l r w y), and it is mostly from listeners' judgements.
5. **"Approximants feel calm" is the authors' suggestion, not a finding.** Wording: 「研究者推测：这些音听起来平和」
   (L10).
6. **Bouba/kiki is not universal, and Mandarin is an exception.** The 95 % comes from Ramachandran & Hubbard 2001
   and no sample is described (S1). The 25-language study found 72 %, and **Mandarin speakers were one of three
   groups below 50 %** (S2, S3). For a Chinese audience, don't say "全世界的人都会把kiki配尖的". If used at all:
   「多数语言的人会这样配，但说普通话的人，并没有明显这样选」.
7. **Chinese and the paper:** Chinese was a listener group (37 people, effect present) and a stimulus language
   (2 pairs). Chinese swear words were **not** in the pilot count. Nobody has tested whether Mandarin swear words
   avoid approximants (C1-C5). Don't say "中文脏话也符合这个规律".
8. **Tone vs word:** Cross-cultural recognition of anger in voices is well supported, but accuracy is lower across
   cultures than within one (in-group advantage, T3, T4). Use "听得出来" for the tone, not "百分百听得出".
9. **Pain results:** Swearing made people last longer in ice water (pain *tolerance*). Whether it hurts less
   (*perceived* pain) is inconsistent across studies (P4). Wording: 「骂一句，冰水里能多撑一会儿」, not 「骂人能止痛」.
   The review calling the effect "reliable" is co-authored by the effect's own discoverer (Stephens).
10. **Brain:** "Aphasia patients can often still swear" is widely stated, but the peer-reviewed primary papers
    (Van Lancker & Cummings 1999; Speedie et al. 1993) were blocked here. What is verified is Broca's Tan (one
    patient, B1). Wording: 「1861年，一位失去语言的病人，只剩一个音节——和一句脏话」.
11. **No real swear word should be spelled out or heard clearly.** See Risks. The paper's attention-check words
    were *fuck/tuck* and *shit/sit*. Don't use them on screen.

## 4. Risks

- **Platform (highest).** Douyin's 2025 notice lists 「恶意制造脏话污语」. The industry code says 「使用粗俗语言」 must be cut,
  and the variety-show code lists **bleeping a vulgar word for emphasis** as a problem (D3-D4). How Douyin's
  automatic review treats foreign-language swearing in audio is not public (UNVERIFIED). Safest build:
  (a) round 2 uses only **pseudo-words**, exactly as the paper did (yog / tsog etc.);
  (b) round 1 (angry tone) uses angry delivery of **neutral or nonsense** words, like Scherer's "Hat sundig pron you
  venzy." The tone effect does not need a real swear word;
  (c) no real swear word (any language, including the paper's fuck/tuck check) is spelled out, subtitled or
  clearly audible; no 谐音 of Chinese swear words; no bleep gags;
  (d) the minced-oath example *darn / damn* is mild and can stay as text.
  The title 「为什么你听得出哪句是脏话？」 contains 脏话 as a topic word, which is normal use, but the platform's
  handling of it is not known.
- **Misleading-claim risk.** "Most people guess right" does not come from the paper (caveat 1). Bouba/kiki is
  "universal" is false for Mandarin speakers (S3). "Swearing kills pain" is overstated (P4). "Chinese swear words
  follow the rule" has no source (C2, C5).
- **Languages and nationalities.** Don't rank languages as "dirtier" or "harsher" (e.g. joking about German or
  French). The paper's French result is a curiosity, not a judgement. Pseudo-words in Arabic, Hindi, Tamil and others
  should not be voiced as caricatures.
- **Real people.** Broca's patient Leborgne (died 1861, now identified by name in historical research) is fine
  as a respectful story; don't mock him. Tourette syndrome: only a small minority have coprolalia. Don't use it as a
  gag, and don't say "Tourette = swearing".
- **AI voice.** The voices are ElevenLabs. Declare AI-generated content on upload (owner's standing rule). This is
  also a neat honest parallel: the study itself used synthetic voices (L5).
- **Minors.** A swearing topic may be limited for younger viewers. Keep the framing scientific; no encouragement
  to swear ("骂人有益" must not become the takeaway, see P3/P4 wording).
- **Maps.** None needed. If languages are shown on a world map, no borders (owner's rule since ep 《应该没事吧》).
