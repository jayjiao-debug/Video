# ep33 你的童年记忆，可能是假的 — facts (researched 2026-10-20)

| # | Fact | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | 6,641 people (UK survey) described their first memory; **38.6%** dated it to age 2 or younger, 893 to age 1 or younger. Research puts the earliest real memories at about **3–3.5 years**; such early "memories" are likely fictional, built from fragments plus photos and family talk ("a large green pram"). "The person remembering them doesn't know this is fictional." | Akhtar, Justice, Morrison & Conway 2018, *Psychological Science* — APS press release 18 Jul 2018 [1] | ✓ |
| M2 | Reconsolidation: rats recalled a fear memory; blocking protein synthesis in the amygdala right after recall erased it (1 or 14 days after learning). Without recall the same drug did nothing; given 6 h later, nothing. → a recalled memory has to be stored again. | Nader, Schafe & LeDoux 2000, *Nature* 406:722–726 [2] | ✓ |
| M3 | Car-crash film. Exp 1 (N=45): speed estimate with "smashed" **40.8 mph** (≈66 km/h) vs "contacted" **31.8 mph** (≈51 km/h). Exp 2 (N=150, 50/group): a week later "did you see broken glass?" — smashed **32%** (16/50), hit 14%, control **12%** (6/50). "There was no broken glass in the accident." | Loftus & Palmer 1974, *J Verbal Learning & Verbal Behavior* 13:585–589 [3] | ✓ original paper |
| M4 | Lost in the mall: 24 participants, relatives supplied 3 true stories + 1 false (lost in a mall aged ~5); **6 (25%)** reported a full or partial false memory. | Loftus & Pickrell 1995, *Psychiatric Annals* 25:720–725 — as reported in Murphy et al. 2023 [4] | ✓ |
| M5 | Doctored photo: the participant (and a relative) Photoshopped into a hot-air-balloon ride that never happened; **20** adults, **3** interviews (≤2 weeks); **50%** formed complete or partial false memories. | Wade, Garry, Read & Lindsay 2002, *Psychon Bull Rev* 9:597–603 [5] | ✓ abstract |
| M6 | Preregistered replication (Ireland, UCC): **123** participants; coders: **35%** false memory at interview 2 (33 partial, 10 full ≈8%); but only **14%** self-reported remembering; 52% more "believed" it happened. | Murphy, Dawson, Huston, … Loftus, Greene 2023, *Memory* 31(6):818–830 [4] | ✓ paper |
| M7 | Reanalysis of the 2023 transcripts (six core details): participants recalled fewer than two on average; 20% of "full" and 58% of "partial" false memories never recalled being lost; the authors estimate only **5 participants** (≈4%) could reasonably be said to have a false memory (vs 43 coded). | Andrews & Brewin, "Lost in the Mall? Interrogating Judgements of False Memory", *Applied Cognitive Psychology* (DOI 10.1002/acp.70012) [6]; Scientific American, Jul 2025 [7] | ✓ (year of print to confirm) |
| M8 | Challenger, Jan 1986: students wrote how they heard the news the day after; ~2.5 years later, mean consistency 2.95/7; **11 of 40** changed every answer; confidence stayed high. | Neisser & Harsch 1992 — as reported by MIT Press Reader [8] | ✓ secondary |
| M9 | 9/11 flashbulb memories over 10 years: consistency fell, confidence stayed high. | MIT Press Reader [8] (Hirst et al.) | ✓ secondary |
| M10 | **375** DNA exonerations in the US (count to 2020); **69%** involved eyewitness misidentification. | Innocence Project [9][10] | ✓ |
| M11 | (optional) Ronald Cotton: picked out of a lineup by a confident witness in 1984, served **10.5 years**, cleared by DNA in 1995; the witness and Cotton later became friends and co-wrote *Picking Cotton* (2009). | Innocence Project case page [11] | ✓ (keep to these facts only) |
