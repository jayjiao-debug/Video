**Corrections to the brief (read first)**
1. **The Egyptian *Dispute* is older than the Siduri tablet, but it is not "c. 1900 BC".** The Berlin museum dates papyrus P 3024 to **about 1850 BC** (1853–1806 BC). The Gilgamesh Sippar tablet is **18th century BC** (Old Babylonian, 18th–17th c.). So the Egypt stop comes first in time (EG1, GB1).
2. **On the Old Babylonian tablet she has no name.** The text calls her "the ale-wife" (*sābītum*), whom George describes as "an old goddess who keeps a tavern". The name Siduri belongs to the later version (not opened here). On screen, say 「酒馆老板娘」 and mention 西杜丽 at most once (GB2).
3. **The "Sippar/Meissner tablet" is two joined pieces, one in Berlin and one in London.** Meissner bought the Berlin piece from a dealer in Baghdad and published it in 1902. Its provenance is only "reportedly from Sippar" (GB1).
4. **The last line, "such is the destiny of mortal men", is partly restored.** The words in brackets are broken on the tablet. It's still the standard reading, but don't show the cuneiform as if it were complete (GB2).
5. **The Buddha's four sights are not in the early canon's account of Gotama.** In the Pāli canon (AN 3.38) the Buddha remembers his palaces and how his "intoxication with youth" dropped away when he thought about old age, but there are no chariot rides and no four sights. Present the four sights as 「传说」 (BU2). His dates are disputed by about 80 years (BU1).
6. **Nietzsche did not write "He who has a why to live can bear almost any how".** That English comes from **Frankl's** book, quoting Nietzsche. Nietzsche's German: 「Hat man sein warum? des Lebens, so verträgt man sich fast mit jedem wie?」 ("if you have your why of life, you get along with almost any how"). He wrote it in **Sils-Maria, Switzerland, in late summer 1888**. It came out in Leipzig at **the end of January 1889**, by which time he had already collapsed (NI1–NI3).
7. **Frankl dictated the book in nine days.** The Viktor Frankl Institut puts this under **1946**. The first edition's title was *Ein Psycholog erlebt das Konzentrationslager*; the famous *…trotzdem Ja zum Leben sagen* came later (FR1–FR2). The exact wording of his "three ways to meaning" was **not verified from the book**, only from the Frankl Institute of America's paraphrase (FR4).
8. **Socrates' line comes from Plato's *Apology*, Plato's version of the defence speech, not a court transcript.** Attribute it to 「苏格拉底在法庭上说（柏拉图记载）」 (SO1).
9. **The tent on the Danube is not documented.** The *Meditations* has two place notes: "Among the Quadi at the Granua" and "This in Carnuntum". No source says he wrote in a tent. The field-camp image is fine as an image, but don't caption it 「帐篷里」 (MA2).
10. **Harvard start year: use 「1938年起」.** The Harvard Gazette and Waldinger say 1938, but HMS Magazine says 1939 (HA1).

**What we can say on screen (true for all sources):**
- 「人类最早写下这个问题的文字，有近四千年了。」 ✓ (F1 early 2nd millennium BC; F2 c. 1850 BC.)
- 「现存最早认真问"活着值不值"的文字之一，是一个无名埃及人写的。」 ✓
- 「最早写下的回答之一，来自一位酒馆老板娘。」 ✓ (She is a fictional character in a poem. Say 「诗里的」 once.)

**What we cannot say:**
- 「第一个问这个问题的人是X」. The question is surely older than writing, the authors are anonymous, and the oldest witness may simply be the one that survived.
- 「吉尔伽美什问了这个问题」 as history. Gilgamesh may have been a real king of Uruk, but these are poems written centuries later (not opened, so treat as literature).
- 「西杜丽说」 as if it were a recorded person.

**Suggested hook wording:** 「人类历史上，第一个写下"人活着为了什么"的，是谁？」 → answer 「没人知道他的名字。」 (honest and stronger). Avoid 「第一个问的人」 in the title; 「最早写下」 is safe.

## Story-worthy

1. **The oldest written answer is the newest one.** About 3,800 years ago the ale-wife told Gilgamesh: eat, wash, gaze on the little one who holds your hand, let your wife delight in your embrace, "such is the destiny of mortal men" (GB2). Harvard's 88-year study: "Good relationships keep us happier and healthier" (HA3). That's the bookend, and it's real.
2. **Ecclesiastes says almost the same words ~1,500 years later.** Bread with joy, white garments, oil on the head, "enjoy life with the wife whom thou lovest", "that is your portion" (EC2). Scholars have called it a "striking parallel" since Grimme (GB4). The book famous for 「虚空的虚空」 ends up at the same table as the tavern-keeper.
3. **Nietzsche's famous line isn't his wording.** He wrote "if you have your why, you get along with almost any how", and then joked that only the English chase happiness. The version everyone quotes comes from Frankl, who carried it through the camps (NI1, NI3). The book came out in January 1889, when Nietzsche had already collapsed in Turin (NI2).
4. **The emperor didn't want to get out of bed either.** Marcus Aurelius: "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly… I am rising to the work of a human being" (MA3). It was written to himself, in Greek, with notes saying where: "Among the Quadi at the Granua" (MA2).
5. **Thirty votes.** Socrates: "had thirty votes gone over to the other side, I should have been acquitted" (SO2). His last words were about a debt, a rooster owed to the god of healing (SO3).
- Bonus: **Zhuangzi did cry first.** 「是其始死也，我独何能无概然！」 Then he saw her life as the four seasons turning and stopped (ZH2).
- Bonus: **Pascal's book was never a book.** It was strips of paper threaded on string, found in no order (PA1).
- Bonus: the 1938 Harvard sophomores included **John F. Kennedy** (HA4). In 1938 the researchers expected cholesterol to predict old age, not love (HA3).

**Strongest human story:** Viktor Frankl, a Vienna doctor, married Tilly in 1941. In 1942 both were deported with his parents. On a dawn march, stars fading, he held on to his wife's image: "her look was then more luminous than the sun which was beginning to rise", and "The salvation of man is through love and in love" (FR3). Liberated on 27 April 1945, he learned that his wife, mother and brother were dead (FR1). In 1946 he dictated the book in nine days (FR2). It's the same answer the ale-wife gave, found in the worst place on earth.

**Best visual image:** the hand. A small hand holding a bigger one, pressed in clay 3,800 years ago (the cuneiform for 「牵着你手的孩子」, iii.12). Then the papyrus roll, the cup, the basin, the threaded paper strips, the fading stars over a marching column. The 3 a.m. phone screen in the opening dissolves into that tablet.

## Recommended order (9 stops + bookend), and why

0. **Today, 3 a.m.** A phone types 「人活着到底为了什么」. The viewer's own question opens the film.
1. **c. 1850 BC, Egypt: a man argues with his own soul** (EG1). The question in its rawest form, the 3 a.m. feeling, from an anonymous poet: "you're not the first".
2. **c. 1800 BC, Babylonia: Gilgamesh runs from death; the ale-wife answers** (GB1–GB3). Show only "You cannot find the life that you seek" and **hold back the rest** for the ending. It plants the payoff.
3. **c. 500 BC, Lu: 「未知生，焉知死？」** (CO1). The first turn from death back to life. Familiar to the Chinese audience, so it builds trust early.
4. **399 BC, Athens: Socrates and the cup** (SO1–SO3). The answer becomes "examine your life". Strong prop (cup) and stakes (30 votes).
5. **Warring States: Zhuangzi drumming on the basin** (ZH1–ZH2). The emotional low-to-high: grief, then the four seasons. The most cinematic Chinese moment.
6. **c. 3rd c. BC, Jerusalem: 「虚空的虚空」 → 9:7–9** (EC1–EC2). Despair at full volume, then the twist: "eat your bread with joy… with the wife you love". It's the first audible echo of the ale-wife.
7. **AD 170s, the Danube front: Marcus Aurelius gets out of bed** (MA1–MA3). Duty: the most powerful man alive gives himself a pep talk at dawn, which mirrors the 3 a.m. viewer.
8. **1888 → 1946: Nietzsche's "why", carried by Frankl into the camps** (NI1–NI3, FR1–FR3). One baton, two stops. It's the darkest place and the clearest answer: love.
9. **1938 → today: Harvard's 724 people** (HA1–HA4). The science arrives at the same place: relationships, not cholesterol.
10. **Bookend: back to the clay.** Now show the full ale-wife lines: 「看着那个牵着你手的孩子……这就是人的命运。」 Then cut to the phone at 3 a.m. Comment question: 「你活着，是为了谁？」

Cut and why: **Buddha** (the four sights are a later legend, there's a religious-sensitivity and Nepal/India map risk, and it adds a third "death" beat). **Pascal** (strong prop, but the thinking reed doesn't move the "love/relationships" throughline). Either can come back as a 2-second montage card. Camus: at most one line (brief).

## Disputed and open items

- **Who asked first:** see B. Use 「最早写下」, never 「第一个问」.
- **Dispute date:** museum c. 1850 BC vs brief c. 1900 BC → use the museum's.
- **Sippar tablet date:** 18th c. BC (ETANA) vs 18th–17th c. (George) → say 「公元前18世纪前后」 / 「三千多年前」, or 「近四千年前」 only for the relay as a whole.
- **Siduri's name** isn't on the OB tablet (correction 2). Her advice being *removed* from the later version is UNVERIFIED (GB5).
- **Buddha's dates:** 560–480 vs died c. 405 BCE vs 563/480 birth → 「约两千五百年前」.
- **Nietzsche:** his wording vs Frankl's quotation, and the collapse on 3 vs 7 Jan 1889 → 「1889年1月」.
- **Frankl:** the nine days are under 1946 (Institut). The exact three-ways wording and the German originals are UNVERIFIED.
- **Harvard:** 1938 vs 1939 → 「1938年起」.
- **Not opened, so don't state as fact:** Confucius 551–479 BC; Zhuangzi's dates; 《至乐》 authorship; Pascal d. 1662; the Agora prison building; the jury size (501); κύλιξ; the "tent"; Granua = Hron; published Chinese translations other than 杨伯峻, 何兆武 and 和合本.

## Risks

- **Suicide (high, platform).** The Egyptian *Dispute* is about choosing death, and the 3 a.m. search 「人活着到底为了什么」 is something a person in crisis types. Never show or suggest a method. Frame EG1 as 「和灵魂吵了一架」, not 「想自杀」. Have the narrative answer quickly. Recommend the producer add a crisis-line card in the description or end card; the **hotline number must be taken from an official source** (not verified here).
- **Death scenes.** Socrates: cup and lying down, no convulsions. Zhuangzi: the wife's death off-screen. Frankl: **no gas chambers, corpses or Nazi symbols** (swastika is a hard platform block). Use the dawn march and stars only.
- **Religion.** Ecclesiastes is scripture and the Buddha is a religious founder. Present them as historical texts ("this book says"), without preaching. For the Buddha, say 「传说」 (BU2). The 和合本 is a church translation. Quoting a verse is fine; no proselytising captions.
- **Maps.** Don't draw national borders, especially for India/Nepal (the Buddha's birthplace is sensitive) or any map of China (needs a complete official map with 审图号). Safest: no outlines, place names only.
- **Real people.** Waldinger and Schulz are alive: quote only their published words (HA2–HA3). JFK as a participant is in Harvard's own article. The Frankl family deaths need a respectful tone.
- **Translations and quotes.** Several Chinese lines are 本片译. Label them as such in the posting copy if asked. Don't attribute them to 吕娜, 何怀宏, 陈鼓应, 严群 or 王太庆, since those weren't opened. The commonly shared English "He who has a why…" must not be captioned as Nietzsche's own words.
- **Audio and licences.** SOAS BAPLAR recordings (Akkadian read aloud) are tempting for the "place's own language" VO. Check their licence first (not checked). For TTS: Akkadian, Middle Egyptian and Classical Greek have no reliable TTS, so use modern-language VO with the ancient text on screen.
