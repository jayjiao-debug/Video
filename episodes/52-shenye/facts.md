## Story-worthy

1. **Three completely different datasets — government mortality records, a mental-health forum, and half a billion tweets — all independently point to the same stretch of the night.** US suicide-death records (adjusted for who's actually awake) peak at 2–3 AM; Reddit's r/SuicideWatch posts peak 2–5 AM while the control subreddit peaks in the evening; and ordinary Twitter negative-affect language climbs steadily into the night. None of these researchers were looking for the same thing, and they all land in the same few hours. That convergence, not any single number, is the strongest "this is real" beat for the film.
2. **The 60% amygdala number (Yoo & Gujar, Walker lab, 2007).** A single sleepless night makes the brain's fear/threat center react 60% more strongly to the same negative image, while its connection to the "reasoning" prefrontal cortex weakens — an actual scan of two different brain states reacting to the identical picture. Extremely visual: same photo, two brains, two reactions.
3. **The forced-desynchrony experiment (row 4) proves it isn't just "you're tired" — it's your clock.** Scientists kept people's total sleep the same but shifted *when* they slept through a 28-hour "day," and mood still cratered when their body clock said it was nighttime. This directly rebuts the "you're just overthinking/being dramatic" narrative the episode wants to counter.
4. **Night owls get a double dose of negative feeling** (Golder & Macy's own finding, buried in their supplementary data): unlike everyone else, people who are most active late at night show negative affect peaking *twice* — once in the morning and again late at night. If the audience skews toward night owls, this is a very "this is literally me" detail.
5. **Stimulants don't fix it.** In the Iowa Gambling Task sleep-deprivation studies, caffeine and even prescription stimulants (modafinil, dextroamphetamine) restored alertness but did *not* restore good decision-making — a counterintuitive, concrete fact that undercuts "just have more coffee and power through."

**Strongest human story:** the Reddit r/SuicideWatch dataset (row 7) — real, anonymous people, writing in real time, at the exact hours the mortality data says is most dangerous, while a neutral comparison forum (AskReddit) is busiest at a completely different, ordinary evening hour. It's the clearest "the data has a face" moment without needing any individual's identifiable story.

**Best visual image:** the forced-desynchrony protocol (row 4) — the same person's mood tracked as their sleep schedule rotates all the way around a 28-hour "day" while nothing else changes; visually this is a clock/dial slowly rotating against a mood line that dips every time it crosses the person's biological night, independent of how much they slept. Alternatively: the split-screen amygdala scan from row 1/2 (same stimulus, two brain states).

## Risks

- **Rows 6 and 7 are suicide-mortality and suicide-crisis-forum data.** This is the single highest-risk material in the episode. It must never be presented as a standalone "shock stat" or as if nighttime itself is dangerous in a way that could read as suggestion or normalization. Required counterweights per the brief:
  - Always pair any mention of the nighttime suicide-risk data with the explicit, immediate point that this is exactly *why* the finding is useful/hopeful: the danger is tied to a temporary brain state (sleep-deprived, circadian-night), not to the truth of the person's situation — "this feeling is real, but it is not the full picture, and it predictably changes by morning."
  - Never state the statistic in a way that could be read as "here's when people most often ___" as a how-to or normalization; keep the frame on "this is why professionals tell you not to trust 3am conclusions," not on the act itself.
  - End the episode with a concrete, actionable, non-vague "what to do" (rows 11/12: write it down, wait for daylight, reach out to someone) so the film never ends on the dark fact alone.
  - If the episode discusses crisis-line data at all (row 8), consider including or signposting a helpline in the end card, consistent with standard responsible-reporting practice for suicide-adjacent content (always pair data with a resource, never describe method, never sensationalize).
- **Do not medicalize normal sadness.** Per the brief: most people feeling low at 2am are experiencing a normal, well-documented circadian/sleep-debt effect, not a disorder. The script should repeatedly distinguish "your brain's temporary nighttime state" from "something being wrong with you" — the whole point of the film is de-stigmatizing, not diagnosing.
- **The "emo hour" / precise clock-time temptation (row 14).** The hook ("凌晨2点，你又开始想那个人了") is emotionally resonant and happens to sit inside the real data's danger window — but the three sources don't all point to the same single minute, and none of them studied "thinking about an ex" specifically. Keep the hook as a relatable scene-setter, not a claim that research proved "2am is when you think about exes."
- **Don't overclaim "data proves texting your ex / online shopping are caused by this."** Rows 3, 10 and 13 are the weakest links between the real neuroscience and the specific relatable behaviors (texting an ex, impulse-buying) the brief wants to open with. The honest claim is "the brain mechanisms that would normally stop you from acting on a risky/emotional impulse are measurably weaker late at night/sleep-deprived" (rows 1–3, 10) — not "science proved you will text your ex at 2am" or any specific e-commerce percentage (row 13, explicitly UNVERIFIED).
- **Platform/fact-check risk:** rows 6–8 involve real mortality and crisis-service data; keep exact source citations (DOI, organization report) ready in case Douyin's fact-check team asks for evidence, per qa-lessons.md. No maps, no identifiable real individuals, no screenshots of actual Reddit/Crisis Text Line posts.
- **No real screenshots or recognizable platform UI** (actual Reddit post text, actual Crisis Text Line conversations) — use only the aggregate statistics, never reproduce or dramatize an individual's real crisis post.
